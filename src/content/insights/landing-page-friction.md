A landing page can look polished and still make a qualified prospect hesitate. The problem is often not a dramatic design failure. It is a small disconnect between the promise, proof and next action.

This guide covers five friction points that can be checked without pretending there is one universal “high-converting” layout.

> This is practical conversion guidance, not a guarantee. Test changes against qualified outcomes, not only clicks or form completions.

## The short answer

Review the page in the same order a visitor experiences it:

1. Can they recognise the offer they clicked?
2. Can they tell who it is for—and who it is not for?
3. Is the primary next step obvious?
4. Does the proof explain a relevant result and context?
5. Does the form ask only for information the process will use?
6. Can the same decision be made comfortably on mobile?

Google describes landing-page experience in terms that include usefulness, relevance, navigation and whether the page meets expectations created by the ad. Its [Google Ads optimisation guidance](https://support.google.com/google-ads/answer/6238826/optimize-your-ads-and-landing-pages?hl=en-GB) also recommends message consistency, simple navigation, mobile usability and useful original content.

## 1. The promise changes after the click

An ad may offer “Google Ads lead-quality audit for Malaysian service businesses,” then send the visitor to a general digital-marketing page. The business may provide that service, but the visitor has to reconstruct the connection.

Keep the central promise continuous:

- use the same offer and audience language;
- show any important eligibility, price or location conditions;
- repeat the action the ad asked the person to take; and
- avoid introducing several unrelated services before resolving the original question.

This does not require copying the ad word for word. It requires the page to make the next step feel like a continuation, not a new research task.

## 2. The next action is unclear

Several equal buttons create a decision the campaign did not intend. “Call,” “WhatsApp,” “Download,” “Book,” “View services” and “Contact us” may all be valid, but displaying them with equal weight can obscure the primary path.

Choose one main action for the page's intent. Secondary actions can remain available with lower visual priority.

The label should describe what happens. “Request an audit” is clearer than “Submit.” Beside the action, explain the next step: who will respond, through which channel, and the expected timing. Do not promise a response time the business cannot maintain.

## 3. Proof lacks context

A logo strip, testimonial or percentage can look authoritative while answering very little. Useful proof helps the visitor judge relevance.

For a case study, explain:

- the type of business and market;
- the initial constraint;
- Wayne Omni's actual role;
- what changed;
- the period measured; and
- any important limitation or missing metric.

Only publish numbers that can be supported and approved. If commercial details are confidential, explain the work and decision process without inventing a result. A modest, specific statement is more trustworthy than an unsupported superlative.

## 4. The form asks before trust is built

A form creates effort and a privacy decision. Every field should support qualification, routing, consent or a useful first reply.

Ask of each field:

- Will someone use this information before the first response?
- Is it necessary at this stage?
- Does the label make the reason clear?
- Could a less sensitive or easier answer work?
- Is any optional field honestly marked optional?

Place the form after enough information to understand the offer, or pair it with concise proof and next-step expectations. Keep relevant privacy and consent information close to the fields it explains. For promotional WhatsApp follow-up, review the distinction between a current enquiry and [future messaging permission](/insights/whatsapp-opt-in-malaysia/).

## 5. Mobile comparison is harder than desktop

Responsive layout is not the same as an easy mobile decision. A page can technically fit the screen while hiding key information behind long sections, cramped cards or a sticky button that covers content.

Test on a real phone and check:

- the headline and offer are readable without zooming;
- key proof appears before fatigue sets in;
- tap targets are distinct and do not overlap;
- the form uses appropriate input types;
- error messages appear beside the affected field;
- comparison tables remain understandable; and
- the page loads acceptably on a normal mobile connection.

Google Ads provides a [landing-page report](https://support.google.com/google-ads/answer/7543502?hl=en) for reviewing landing-page performance and mobile optimisation signals. Use it as one diagnostic input, then validate the actual journey and qualified outcomes.

## Use page-experience metrics as diagnostics

A visitor cannot evaluate the offer comfortably if the main content arrives late, the page ignores an interaction or the layout moves while they are trying to read. Core Web Vitals give those technical problems shared definitions.

Google's current [Core Web Vitals guidance](https://web.dev/articles/vitals?hl=en) recommends evaluating the 75th percentile of page loads, separated by mobile and desktop. A “good” result is:

- Largest Contentful Paint (LCP) at or below 2.5 seconds;
- Interaction to Next Paint (INP) at or below 200 milliseconds; and
- Cumulative Layout Shift (CLS) at or below 0.1.

These thresholds are not conversion guarantees. They help distinguish technical friction from message, proof or offer problems. Review both field data—which reflects real visits when enough data exists—and controlled tests that help reproduce a specific issue.

Keep the diagnosis connected to the customer journey. A slow hero image may delay the promise. An unresponsive menu may hide essential proof. A moving form can cause an accidental tap. Record the user-facing consequence alongside the technical metric so the fix is prioritised by impact, not only by a score.

## A practical landing-page review

Use one real campaign and review the page in context:

1. Capture the ad, keyword or social post that sends traffic.
2. Open the final URL on desktop and mobile.
3. Write down the audience, promise, proof and primary action shown in each.
4. Complete the form or message path with a controlled test lead.
5. Confirm the lead reaches the correct system with useful source context.
6. Ask a person unfamiliar with the page what they think will happen next.
7. Change one meaningful source of friction and compare qualified outcomes over a reasonable period.

Do not declare a winner from a tiny sample. Seasonality, campaign mix and lead-assessment delay can distort a simple before-and-after comparison.

## What marketers often get wrong

- **Adding copy without improving order.** More detail can bury the answer if the hierarchy remains unclear.
- **Removing every form field.** Fewer fields can increase submissions while reducing the context needed for a useful reply.
- **Using vague testimonials.** “Great service” does not explain fit, scope or result.
- **Copying a competitor's layout.** Their traffic, offer, proof and sales process may be different.
- **Optimising only for submission rate.** A page that attracts more unqualified enquiries may create more work without more value.

## Frequently asked questions

### How many calls to action should a landing page have?

There is no universal number. A focused campaign page usually benefits from one primary action repeated where useful, with secondary contact options visually subordinate. The action should match the campaign's promise and the team's ability to respond.

### Should the form be above the fold?

Only when the visitor has enough context to act confidently. A familiar, low-risk offer may need little explanation. A complex or high-consideration service usually needs proof, constraints and next-step clarity near the form.

### Does a shorter landing page convert better?

Not inherently. Length should match the decision. Remove repetition and irrelevant detail, but keep the information needed to understand fit, trust the provider and take the next step.

### What should I measure?

Track page actions, completed enquiries, qualified leads and downstream outcomes. Also monitor form errors, device differences and response time. A stronger page should improve the quality and clarity of the journey, not just increase button clicks.
